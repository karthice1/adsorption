## Appendix A. Coverage guarantee of the study-level split-conformal interval (M2s)

**Setting.** Let the studies used in one leave-one-study-out fold be divided at random into a *proper-training* set and $J$ *calibration* studies, and let $Z_{J+1}$ be the held-out test study. Each study is a set of observations,

$$Z_j=\{(x_{ji},y_{ji}):\ i=1,\dots,n_j\}.$$

The study size $n_j$ is part of the study: it is random, can vary between studies, and the observations within a study may be arbitrarily dependent. Let $\hat\mu$ be a model fitted to the proper-training studies only. For a fixed $\beta\in(0,1]$ define:

- the residuals $R_{ji}=|y_{ji}-\hat\mu(x_{ji})|$;
- the study score $s_j$, the $k_j$-th smallest of the residuals $R_{ji}$ of study $j$, with $k_j=\lceil\beta n_j\rceil$.

For a miscoverage level $\alpha$, let $k=\lceil(1-\alpha)(J+1)\rceil$. Set $\hat q=s_{(k)}$, the $k$-th smallest of the $J$ calibration scores, if $k\le J$, and $\hat q=\infty$ otherwise. The M2s interval at any $x$ is $\hat\mu(x)\pm\hat q$.

**Assumption.** Conditional on the proper-training studies, $Z_1,\dots,Z_J,Z_{J+1}$ are exchangeable. Independence is not required. The simulated corpora do not consist of independent draws from one population (see "Exchangeability under the simulator design" below); in this paper the assumption is satisfied by the random assignment of roles to the studies of a corpus.

**Theorem.** Under the assumption,

$$P\Big(\#\{i:\ |y_{J+1,i}-\hat\mu(x_{J+1,i})|\le\hat q\}\ \ge\ \lceil\beta n_{J+1}\rceil\Big)\ \ge\ 1-\alpha .$$

In words: with probability at least $1-\alpha$, at least $\lceil\beta n_{J+1}\rceil$ of the $n_{J+1}$ observations of the test study lie inside its intervals, so its within-study coverage is at least $\lceil\beta n\rceil/n\ge\beta$. If the scores are almost surely distinct, the probability is also at most $1-\alpha+1/(J+1)$.

**Proof.**
1. $\hat\mu$ does not depend on $Z_1,\dots,Z_{J+1}$, and every score is the same measurable function of its study. Hence $s_1,\dots,s_{J+1}$ are exchangeable.
2. By the standard quantile lemma of split conformal prediction (Vovk et al., 2005; Lei et al., 2018), the rank of $s_{J+1}$ among the $J+1$ scores, with ties broken at random, is uniform on $\{1,\dots,J+1\}$. Therefore $P(s_{J+1}\le s_{(k)})\ge k/(J+1)\ge 1-\alpha$.
3. On the event $s_{J+1}\le\hat q$, by the definition of $s_{J+1}$ as an order statistic, at least $\lceil\beta n_{J+1}\rceil$ residuals of the test study are $\le s_{J+1}\le\hat q$.
4. The upper bound follows from the uniform rank when the scores are distinct. ∎

In this paper the test study is a randomly selected held-out study from the same realised corpus, and the probability is over the random assignment of roles, marginally over the study's material family and study size (next paragraph). It is a finite-corpus randomisation guarantee, not a conventional distribution-free guarantee for future experimental studies: the theorem says nothing about a genuinely new study drawn from an independent literature population.

**Exchangeability under the simulator design.**
- *Design.* A corpus has 30 studies. Material families are assigned in a fixed rotation (study $i$ has family $i$ mod 6, five studies per family); they are not randomised. The family hyper-priors are drawn once per corpus and shared by all its studies; materials and study conditions are then drawn independently per study. Studies of different families are therefore not identically distributed, and studies of one corpus are dependent through the shared hyper-priors. For a total-Cr endpoint the corpus is restricted to the $N$ studies that report total Cr.
- *Role assignment.* In the evaluation each of the $N$ studies is the test study in turn, with equal weight, and the remaining $N-1$ are split at random into $P$ proper-training and $J$ calibration studies, the split being drawn independently of which study is the test study. Over the test index and the split, the triple (test study, proper-training set, calibration set) is a uniformly random partition of the $N$ studies into sets of sizes 1, $P$ and $J$.
- *Proposition.* Fix a realised corpus. Conditional on the proper-training set, the test study is a uniformly random member of the remaining $J+1$ studies. Hence the assumption holds conditionally on the corpus, and the theorem gives $P(\text{coverage}\ge\beta\mid\text{corpus})\ge 1-\alpha$, where the probability is over the role assignment. Averaging over corpora gives the marginal statement.
- *Proof.* All partitions with the given sizes are equally likely. Given the proper-training set, each of the $J+1$ remaining studies is the test study in exactly the same number of them. A uniformly random member of a fixed set of $J+1$ studies, whose scores are computed by a model that does not depend on them, has an exchangeable score among the $J+1$ scores. ∎
- *Answers to the questions this raises.* (i) The family allocation is fixed, not random; the proof does not use it. (ii) Calibration and test studies are exchangeable conditional on the proper-training set, and on the corpus, because of the random role assignment. (iii) Shared family random effects change the *distribution* of the scores, so that, for example, the scores of one family can be systematically larger, but not their exchangeability under random role assignment; they make family-conditional coverage differ from the marginal level. (iv) The guarantee is for a study drawn at random from the corpus that supplies the training and calibration studies, i.e. from the same realised hierarchical population. It is not a guarantee for a study from a new, independent corpus with different hyper-prior draws, nor for a new study whose family is chosen non-randomly. (v) When the test study belongs to a particular family, including one that is rare or absent among the calibration studies, the guarantee does not apply conditionally on that family; it is an average over the corpus's family mix.
- *Numerical check (Table 7b, Table S5c).* On the 10 corpora of §3.5, 50 random proper-training sets were drawn per corpus and every one of the $J+1$ remaining studies was taken as test in turn; this is the evaluation reported as the M2s result in §3.5. Because coverage of at least $\lceil\beta n\rceil$ observations is equivalent to $s_{J+1}\le\hat q$, the enumerated within-corpus rate equals $k/(J+1)$ when scores are distinct, at every level: at 90%, 0.933 for the Cr(VI) endpoints in every corpus. This confirms that the implementation realises the role assignment of the proposition; it is not independent evidence. A single fixed-seed realisation, such as the original leave-one-study-out run, scatters around this rate. The informative columns are those the theorem does not cover: coverage conditional on the test study's family (0.85–0.98 for apparent uptake) and coverage for test studies from another corpus (0.93 on average, 0.84 in the worst corpus pair).
- *Family-stratified alternative.* A Mondrian (family-stratified) split conformal would give family-conditional coverage, but with five studies per family and corpus it has at most four calibration studies per family, and its 90% interval is unbounded (it needs at least 9). We therefore keep the marginal guarantee and state its scope.

**Remarks.**
1. *Marginal, not conditional.* The guarantee is marginal over the population of studies, including its mixture of material families and study sizes. It does not hold conditionally within a family. The family-level coverage reported in §3.5 and Table S5 is therefore descriptive.
2. *Effect of small studies.* $\lceil\beta n\rceil/n$ is conservative for small studies. For $\beta=0.9$, a study of 28 observations needs 26 inside (92.9%), and a study of 8 observations needs all 8.
3. *Minimum number of calibration studies.* The interval is finite only if $J\ge(1-\alpha)/\alpha$: at least 9 calibration studies for $\alpha=0.10$ and 19 for $\alpha=0.05$. With half of the 29 training studies used for calibration (about 14), M2s is bounded at 90% and unbounded at 95%. For total-Cr endpoints only about 8 calibration studies are available, so M2s is unbounded even at 90%. This finite-sample limit is reported as a result, not hidden.
4. *Relation to Dunn et al. (2023).* Their methods target coverage of a *single random observation* from a new group, using pooling, subsampling or repeated subsampling. M1 is our analogue of their weighted pooling. M2s targets a different, group-level $(\beta,1-\alpha)$ tolerance statement: a fraction $\beta$ of a new study's observations, with confidence $1-\alpha$.
5. *M2 (CV+) has no proven guarantee.* M2 uses the same study scores, but with K-fold models instead of a single split, as in the CV+ construction (Barber et al., 2021). The CV+ guarantee is proven for scalar residual scores of exchangeable data points. We have not shown that it extends to within-study quantile scores, so no $1-2\alpha$ guarantee is claimed for M2. M2 is reported as an empirical method only.
